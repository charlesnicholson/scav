Speed without changing a drawing

Making the search fast, exactly

Every optimisation here is exact: with or without it, every corpus chart's drawing is byte-identical at both scales.

RuleEach optimisation ships with a test mode that runs the unoptimised computation alongside and compares, and a planted defect proves the comparison can fail. A memo keyed on everything a step reads returns exactly what the step would compute; a sound bound discards only candidates that could never be taken.

Before any of this, one no-text layout of bottler scored 213,768 candidates, 94% of which lost. Brandes–Köpf ran 19.8 million times for 69,496 distinct graphs, because a candidate differs from the one before it in a single frame. Sizing was 82% of the time, and 57% of samples were in the allocator. Every fix below uses one fact: a candidate is the incumbent plus one pin, so almost everything it computes has been computed before.

A general lower bound does not help inside the search: area after sizing and a bend floor never reach 80% of the incumbent's Tier 2 on any candidate, while 58% of losers land within 1% of it. The label bound below works because it bounds only the label half of the cost.

Frame memos and interned keys

Each thread keeps three memos: Brandes–Köpf keyed on the whole graph; each frame's sizing, keyed on its nodes, edges, gaps, state sizes, port offsets, labels, ratio, compaction, fold and profile; and each frame's ordering, keyed on the frame as built, the pins inside it, its label charges and the profile. A memo hit emits no trace, so traced runs bypass them. Each is capped at 220 words (4 MiB) per thread. The memos took no-text mill from 506 s to 58 s wall on 12 cores, and bottler from 18.4 s to 2.9 s.

The keys are interned. A decomposed graph carries a process-unique serial for itself and its chart, so an ordering key names a frame by (serial, index) instead of spelling the frame out. The profile is one interned word instead of 52. Each transition's label segment is a table on the graph instead of a walk up the tree per box per pass. That saved 6.7% of instructions at real text.

Per-frame ordering and sizing reuse

Even a memo hit has to build and hash a key. Ordering reuse skips that. An ordering records what each frame reads beyond the graph: the profile; each segment's reversal, cut and side pins and label charge; each state's rank pin; and each frame's orientation. A candidate compares its record to the incumbent's frame by frame, before and after the facing pass, orders only the frames that differ, and copies the rest with node indices shifted. No key is built and no memo is probed.

Sizing reuse does the same for coordinates, except that a frame's size depends on its children's, so a candidate re-sizes every frame whose inputs differ and every frame that holds it, deepest first, and copies the rest. Over the corpus and gauntlet, 84.5% of frames are copied, and instructions fell 6.1% at real text.

The face shortcut

Phases 1 and 2 and the facing pass never read a face pin, and face moves were two thirds of all scored moves. So a face candidate is routed and scored from the incumbent's kept phase-1 and phase-2 state, at about a quarter of the cost of a rank candidate (65 µs against 252 µs on mill). It also keeps the incumbent's labels wherever nothing a box reads has changed: 92% of a face move's boxes on mill.

Two refinements. No-op faces are charged, not scored: a face pin the router reports would not move the seat draws the incumbent exactly, so it is charged to the budget without being scored; that is about a quarter of face moves. Faces are offered only where they are priced (see the move table), a mask the incumbent's own scoring fills once per taken move, never per candidate. At real text only 48% of face candidates are scored, instructions fell 16.4%, and every drawing stayed byte-identical.

The label bound: route first, label best-first

Labels are placed last and read only the routes, so before any label is placed, every route term, adjacency and the six route-only Tier 0 counts are already final. A round with labels runs in two passes:

  1. Route every candidate in parallel without labels and score a lower bound. The label terms are zero, area and whitespace are measured over the unlabelled chart, and aspect is dropped unless every label box fits inside the sized chart.
  2. Visit the candidates whose bound beats the incumbent, least bound first (ties by enumeration index). Place labels and score each exactly, but only while its bound can still beat the best exact cost so far, or tie it at a lower index. Stop at the first that cannot.

The pick is exactly what scoring every candidate whole would give, and a test mode scores every bounded round whole and compares. At real text, 91% of candidates never place their labels, and instructions fell 35.5% (mill 4,002 G → 2,613 G). Two variants lost: keeping every candidate's routes saved 5% more instructions but cost 16% more memory, and labelling 8 or 32 at once cost 5–11% more.

Kept bound geometry. The first pass already routed each candidate, so routing it again for the exact pass is waste. A candidate whose bound is below the incumbent keeps its routes while it is among the 128 least by (bound, index), in a per-search slot swapped with the scorer's scratch so that nothing allocates. Its exact pass restores the chart those routes saw and places labels on them instead of laying out again. On mill, the 128 slots cover 92% of the candidates that get labelled, and save 4.5% of instructions at the same peak memory. Keeping every candidate's routes saves 4.6% but costs 35–42% more memory.

Allocation-free scratch

Ordering, sizing and packing, scored-move pins and candidates, routes, label placement, nudging and coordinate views all fill per-thread buffers that are reused across candidates. On bottler at real text, heap allocations per scored candidate fell 216 → 103 → 53 → 14 → 8.7 → 4.2, and allocations over a whole run fell from 72.2 M to 1.39 M. Ordering scratch is kept per nesting depth, because a thread waiting on a call's shards may itself run a candidate that orders.

The radix A* queue

The router's A* pops nodes in a strict total order, (f, g, node), so any exact priority queue pops the same sequence. The open list is now a radix heap over f, with the entries at the least f in a 4-ary heap on the whole order. A push below the current least lowers it and re-places every entry, because the heuristic is admissible but not consistent at the turn into a fixed end. It executes about 1% more instructions but takes 4–6% fewer cycles (mill real text 834 G → 786 G cycles). Route lengths need a square root, and hardware sqrt is banned for determinism; the integer square root now runs Newton's method from above, starting one shift-only step from the nearest power of two and stopping at the first step that does not fall. It is checked exhaustively over all 32-bit inputs and cut 2.5% of mill's instructions.

One global thread pool

All parallel work goes to one pool. Nested calls (rows, then searches, then candidate rounds) push to it, so no call site divides the host's cores among its children. One bottler search went from 755% to 1,085% of 12 cores; a hierarchical profile shows 8.7–11 of 12 cores busy, 3.5–4.4% idle, and 0.2% of time in locks. The search memo removed searches that ran again unchanged (the second kick schedule, the settling passes): tcp 12.9 → 6.4 s CPU.

Where the time goes nowOn mill at real text (profile at the label-bound commit) kicks take 92% of CPU. Of a candidate: routing 39.6%, cost 19%, the first sizing 15%, re-sizing after the facing pass 11.9%, re-ordering after it 5.4%. A* alone was 14.1% of CPU, 9.9 points of it the heap (before the radix queue). Rejected cuts: capping kick depth, early abandonment of kicks (at best 3.4%, and it does not transfer between scales), lazy steepest descent (2%), sizing-only bounds (0.26%), and budgets that shrink with chart size: "a rule that searches a big chart less is the design conceding the goal".
